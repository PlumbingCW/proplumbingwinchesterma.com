Water service line emergency: what failure looks like

A service line fails underground, so the evidence is indirect. Look for these signs and treat any of them as urgent if water is flowing:

  • Water bubbling up through the lawn, driveway or sidewalk
  • A sudden loss of pressure or no water at all
  • A meter that spins with every fixture off
  • A wet or muddy patch above the line that does not dry
  • Hissing or running water noise near the foundation wall

Close the main shutoff valve inside the house, which is usually near the meter. If water keeps flowing past the meter, the break is on the street side and the town water department needs a call as well. The plumber can help you work out who is responsible for the section that failed.

Where the line runs and who owns what

The service line runs from the water main in the street, through a curb stop or shutoff, to the house and the meter. In many places the town owns the main and part of the line, and the homeowner owns the rest. Winchester's water comes from the MWRA system and the town's own reservoirs, with the Winchester Water Department operating the local system.

Because the boundary matters, ask the Water Department where its responsibility ends before you pay for a repair. A short call can save a large bill. The plumber needs to know that too if excavation will extend into the street. The arrangement is not the same everywhere. Woburn, for example, draws part of its supply from its own wells near Horn Pond, so its water department answers a different set of questions.

Lead service lines and what to check

Older service lines can be lead, galvanized or copper. The MWRA report for Winchester describes lead gooseneck replacement as ongoing work, which is a reminder that old lead components existed in the system. If your house predates the 1950s, find out what material your line is. A magnet and a scratch test at the entry point give a first clue, and the Water Department can say what its records show.

If your line is lead, replacement is the long-term answer. Ask the Water Department about any replacement or rebate program and coordinate the work so the town side and the house side are both addressed in one project.

Galvanized interior pipe also narrows from rust and causes low pressure at every fixture. If your house has it, the answer may be whole house repiping and not just a new service line.

How a service line is repaired or replaced

The plumber locates the line, often with a pipe locator, and digs an access pit at the failure or at both ends. For a localized leak a repair coupling or a short replacement can be enough. For a failed or lead line, full replacement from the street connection to the meter is the cleaner fix.

Trenchless replacement, where a new line is pulled through the old route, avoids a long trench. It needs a pit at each end and pipe in good enough condition to guide the new line. Leak detection is useful before digging so the pit is placed correctly.

Permits and licensed work

State guidance says a local plumbing inspector must issue a permit before plumbing is installed, altered or replaced, and licensed plumbers pull them. Service line work can also involve the Water Department and the Department of Public Works. Ask the plumber who handles each approval, and check the license through the state board before you sign.

In Massachusetts, the state plumbing code at 248 CMR 10.00 sets the rules for installation and repair, and licensing comes from the Board of State Examiners of Plumbers and Gas Fitters. Permit rules vary by job, so ask the Winchester Building Department or your plumber what applies to yours.
