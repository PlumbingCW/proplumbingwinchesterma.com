Sump pump emergency: failure during a storm

A sump pump sits in a pit at the lowest point of the basement. It pushes groundwater out and away from the house. When it fails in heavy rain, water rises through the floor and cove joint, and the damage starts within hours. The steps below limit it:

  1. Check the power. A tripped breaker or GFCI outlet is a common culprit
  2. Look in the pit. If the float is stuck, lift it by hand and see if the pump starts
  3. Move boxes, electronics and furniture off the floor
  4. If water is near outlets or the panel, stay out and call a licensed electrician or plumber
  5. Call the 24/7 line and describe what you see

If the power is out in the storm, a battery backup pump is the only thing that works. If you do not have one, a portable utility pump and a generator may help while you wait.

Most pumps last about seven to ten years. If yours is older than that and runs hard, replace it before storm season. A pump that has been unused for months can fail on first use, so test it by pouring a bucket of water into the pit twice a year.

Installing or upgrading a sump system

A new installation starts with a pit. The plumber cuts the floor, sets a liner and places the pump with a check valve and a discharge pipe. A tight lid reduces humidity and radon, and an alarm lets you know when water is rising too high. The discharge line should carry water away from the foundation.

Where the water goes matters. Ask the Winchester Department of Public Works where sump discharge is allowed. Discharging into the sanitary sewer is prohibited in many communities because it overloads the system, and a plumber can route it to the yard or storm drain according to local rules. This is also a good reason to use a licensed plumber.

Battery backups and water-powered backups

A primary pump needs electricity, and storms cut power. A battery backup pump runs on a deep-cycle battery and takes over when the primary fails or the power is out. Some systems send an alert to a phone. Water-powered backups use city water pressure, but they need a suitable supply and discharge route, and some utilities restrict them.

Backups need maintenance. Batteries last a few years and should be tested. If your basement has finished space, the case for a backup is strong. For basement flooding that is more than a pump can handle, see basement waterproofing for drainage and exterior options.

Sump pumps and sewer ejector pumps

Do not confuse a sump pump with a sewer ejector pump. A sump pump moves groundwater. An ejector pump lifts wastewater from a basement bathroom or laundry to the sewer. They look similar, but they are different systems. If the ejector fails, wastewater has nowhere to go, and the call is more urgent.
