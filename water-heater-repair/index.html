Water heater emergency: leaks, gas smell and scalding water

Three situations cannot wait for a daytime appointment. The first is water pooling around or running from the tank. The second is a gas smell, a yellow flame or soot at a gas heater. The third is water that comes out far too hot, which can mean a failed thermostat or relief valve.

  1. For a leak, close the cold water valve above the tank and switch the electric breaker off or set a gas control to pilot
  2. For a gas smell, do not touch switches, leave the house and call your gas utility or 911 from outside
  3. For overheating, turn the thermostat down and keep people away from the relief valve discharge pipe
  4. Call the 24/7 line once everyone is safe

Gas heaters add one more rule. A pilot that keeps going out can point to a failing thermocouple, a draft problem or a blocked vent. Combustion issues are safety issues, so do not keep relighting it.

No hot water: what the plumber checks

The cause of no hot water depends on the fuel. On an electric tank the usual suspects are a tripped breaker, a failed upper or lower heating element or a bad thermostat. On a gas tank they are a pilot that will not stay lit, a failed thermocouple or flame sensor and a gas control valve problem.

Hot water that runs out too quickly often means a broken dip tube or a burned-out lower element. Lukewarm water points to a failing element or a thermostat set too low. The plumber tests before replacing parts, because a part that was not the cause is money wasted.

The tank is leaking: repair or replace?

Location of the leak matters. Water at the top often comes from a loose fitting, a bad relief valve or a dripping connection that can be repaired. Water from the bottom or the tank seam usually means the steel has corroded through. A corroded tank cannot be patched, and replacement is the only fix.

If the tank is more than about ten years old and leaking from the body, do not spend on repairs. Read the water heater replacement page so you know your options before the plumber arrives. If it is newer and the leak is at a fitting, a repair is realistic.

Gas, electric and hybrid heat pump models

Repair differs by type. Gas units rely on burner assemblies, venting and gas controls. Electric units rely on elements and thermostats. Hybrid heat pump water heaters add a compressor, a fan and condensate drainage, so a repair may involve refrigerant side parts that only some plumbers handle.

Tell the dispatcher the brand and model if you can read it from the label. Many calls about a Honeywell or other brand control valve are solved faster when the plumber arrives with the right part. If you have a tankless unit, the tankless water heater page covers flushing and error codes.

Hard water, sediment and the anode rod

Sediment collects at the bottom of the tank and forces the burner or the lower element to work through a layer of scale. The result is noise, slower heat-up and a shorter tank life. The sacrificial anode rod protects the steel by corroding first. When it is used up, the tank corrodes.

We cannot quote a hardness figure for Winchester from the town. The water comes from the MWRA system and town reservoirs in the Middlesex Fells, and the MWRA community report we reviewed does not list hardness. Test your tap with a hardness strip, or ask the Winchester Water Department for its latest water quality report, and then decide how often to flush the tank.

Keeping a water heater running longer

Flush the tank once a year, test the relief valve gently by lifting its lever and check the anode rod after a few years. Set the thermostat at 120 degrees, which reduces scald risk and mineral buildup. If the heater is in a finished basement, a drain pan with a pipe to a floor drain limits damage when the tank finally fails. The same service reaches Arlington and the neighboring towns.

The rules behind this kind of work come from the Massachusetts plumbing code, 248 CMR 10.00. Plumbers hold licenses issued by the state Board of State Examiners of Plumbers and Gas Fitters, so it is reasonable to ask for a license number and a written scope before any work begins.
