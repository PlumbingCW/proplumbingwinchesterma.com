Wedgemere at a glance

Wedgemere is a National Register historic district in Winchester, listed on July 5, 1989. It covers about 39 acres, bounded by Warren Street to the north, Wildwood and Fletcher Streets to the east and Church Street to the south. Cambridge Street marks the western edge, although properties on it are excluded.

  • Almost all houses date from 1890 to 1920
  • Styles include Queen Anne, Shingle, Colonial Revival and Tudor Revival
  • ZIP code: 01890

How the area took shape

The district's National Register record says a syndicate acquired 75 acres here in 1891, and nearly all of the houses went up over the next three decades. They are described as large houses set back on big grassy lots. The name Wedgemere is also used by one of Winchester's two Zone 1 MBTA Commuter Rail stations on the Lowell Line, a short walk from the Winchester Center station that has its own page here.

Plumbing questions in a district of larger houses

We do not have a survey of the pipes inside Wedgemere houses, and the age of a house does not tell you what is behind its walls. Many houses of this period have been renovated more than once. What a larger house usually does have is more of everything: more bathrooms, more fixtures and longer runs between the water heater and the farthest tap. That shapes the questions worth asking.

Sizing comes first. A tank that suits a two-person household can run short when several showers run in the morning, so water heater replacement starts with how many people live in the house and which fixtures run at once. A plumber can then compare a bigger tank with a tankless unit for that pattern.

Hidden leaks come second. In a large house a slow leak can run behind a wall or under a floor for a while before a stain appears, and leak detection looks for the source before anyone opens a wall. A free check you can do yourself: read the water meter, shut off every fixture, and read it again an hour later. If the dial moved, water is going somewhere.

Questions to ask before work in an older house

  • Which parts of the system are being replaced, and which stay?
  • Does the job need a permit, and who pulls it?
  • What will the written scope and price say before work starts?
  • Where is the main shutoff, and will it be tested during the visit?

A National Register listing is an honor, and by itself it does not control what an owner does inside a private house. Any local review rules come from the town, so if a project touches the outside of the house, ask the town before you plan it. For a new fixture, a layout change or plumbing installation in a renovation, your plumber should tell you what needs a permit.
