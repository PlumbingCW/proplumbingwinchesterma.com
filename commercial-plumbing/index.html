After-hours commercial plumbing emergencies

Commercial plumbing has the same failures as a home, with more at stake. Restaurants lose a night's service when a line backs up. Offices lose a day to a restroom leak. Landlords face tenant complaints and damage that spreads to neighboring units. A fast first response limits all of it.

  1. Close the main water valve, or the valve to the affected area
  2. Turn off power to wet equipment if it is safe to do so
  3. Keep customers and staff out of wet or sewage-affected areas
  4. Call the 24/7 line and give a contact who can let the plumber in

Have the building layout ready. The plumber needs to know where the shutoffs are, where the floor drains and cleanouts sit and whether the line is shared with other tenants.

Drains, grease and restaurants

Grease is the leading cause of commercial line backups. Kitchens that run a grease trap or interceptor need regular cleaning, and the schedule depends on volume. A full trap does not trap anything, and grease passes into the building line. Ask the plumber about cleaning frequency and a written service record.

For recurring backups in a commercial kitchen, a combination of cleaning and hydro jetting clears the line. A camera check shows whether grease is the only problem. Garbage disposals in commercial kitchens add to the load. Ask the local sewer authority about requirements for grease control.

Backflow testing, storm drains and routine service

Backflow preventers keep dirty water from flowing backward into the supply. Many commercial buildings, irrigation systems and fire lines have them, and the water supplier often requires periodic testing by a certified tester. Ask your supplier or the Winchester Water Department what your building needs, and keep the test reports.

Storm drains and catch basins collect leaves and silt, and a clogged one floods a lot. Cleaning before the wet season is cheap compared to cleanup. If your property has a lot of paved area, ask about a regular schedule. Roof drains and downspout connections belong on that list too.

Leak detection in commercial buildings

Commercial leak detection can be more difficult because of larger systems and hidden risers. We keep this honest. Locating a leak in a large building takes time and equipment, and not every call ends in a repair at the first visit. Share building drawings and the water bill history to speed up the search. For mixed-use buildings with apartments over storefronts, tell the dispatcher which units are affected and who can authorize work. Property managers can set up a standing list of contacts and shutoff locations so a night call goes quickly. Keep a log of calls for each building, since patterns often point to a root cause. What to do before a commercial plumber arrives

  • Close the nearest fixture shutoff or the main valve if water is escaping
  • Do not use affected restrooms or sinks until they are checked
  • Keep staff and customers away from wet floors and mark the area
  • Do not pour chemical cleaners into a backed-up drain
  • Note which fixtures are affected and when it began so the plumber can plan

The rules behind this kind of work come from the Massachusetts plumbing code, 248 CMR 10.00. Plumbers hold licenses issued by the state Board of State Examiners of Plumbers and Gas Fitters, so it is reasonable to ask for a license number and a written scope before any work begins.
