How a tankless water heater works

When you open a hot tap, a flow sensor tells the unit to fire a burner or energize elements. Water passes through a heat exchanger and leaves at the set temperature. When the tap closes, the unit stops. There is no standing reservoir, so you do not pay to keep water hot overnight.

The limit is flow rate. Every unit can heat only so many gallons per minute for a given temperature rise. In New England, incoming water is cold in winter, so the temperature rise is larger and the flow a unit can deliver is smaller. A size that works in July may fall short in January.

Tankless flush and descale service

This is the maintenance that people skip and then regret. Minerals precipitate inside the heat exchanger as water is heated, narrowing the passages and cutting efficiency. Error codes, lukewarm water and a unit that cycles on and off are all common signs. Manufacturers call for a periodic flush using a descaling solution and a pump through the service ports.

Many call-ins ask whether the unit needs a service after several years. The answer is usually yes, and often overdue. The plumber isolates the unit, circulates the solution for the time the maker specifies, rinses, cleans the inlet filter and checks the vent. Ask for a record of the settings and any error history.

Tankless repair: common faults

Most faults trace to a handful of causes. A scale-clogged heat exchanger, a dirty inlet screen, a failed flow sensor, a blocked vent or condensate drain on a condensing model, and a worn igniter on gas units. Electric tankless units fail at elements and thermal fuses. The first step is to read the error code on the display.

If the unit is old and the parts are costly, compare the repair to replacement. A tankless unit that is well maintained can last many years, but a neglected unit may not be worth a major repair. The water heater repair page covers standard tanks, and the installation page covers the set-up of new units of any kind.

Under-sink and point-of-use units

A small electric unit under a sink can supply a distant bathroom or a kitchen without a long wait for hot water. These units are modest in output, so they suit a single faucet. They need a dedicated circuit, and many need a new outlet. They are a practical fix for a far-off vanity, not a replacement for a whole-house heater.
